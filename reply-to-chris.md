A "definition" is Wikipedia's own lead sentence lifted verbatim; an "edge" is a wikilink between two in-domain articles; the "foundational" ranking is just in-degree. Which goes straight to your Fable question: **nothing here is unique to Fable, and yes, Codex 5.5 (or a local model) would do the same jobs.** The model only ever does three optional, quarantined things — redraw line diagrams, propose candidate definitions the parser couldn't extract, and triage incoming corrections for good faith. All swappable; the brand was incidental and I let it stand in for the system. That was my error, not a feature.

This also dissolves most of the Fable-specific worries you raised. The scholarly core sends nothing to a hosted model — it's local parsing of a public dump — so routing, distillation carve-outs, and 30-day retention simply don't touch it. The only model touchpoints (diagram redraws, candidate suggestions) can run on any model, a local model, or a human; if a vendor's terms are unacceptable for a deployment, you change the component and the pipeline doesn't notice. Your broader point about transparent model routing and predictability of service is a real concern and worth its own thread — I just don't want our work to depend on the answer, and by design it doesn't. On images — you're not poking a hole, you're restating the rule. We never claim to vectorize photographs, and we shouldn't. The media step picks a strategy by type: existing SVG diagrams get redrawn as our own SVGs; charts get rebuilt in D3 from the underlying data; **photographs get used as-is if they're freely licensed, or omitted — never faked.** Your Rho Ophiuchi NIRCam frame is exactly the case the pipeline refuses to vectorize: it's a NASA/ESA/CSA release, freely usable, so the strategy is "use the original," full stop. Turning a star-forming region into an SVG would be both impossible to do faithfully and impossible to validate, which is precisely why it isn't attempted. Your instinct — line drawings yes, photographs no — is the design rule, not a gap in it. Two honest caveats you should hold us to: a redrawn diagram still has to be checked against the original by a human (ideally a domain expert), and we only attempt the cases where that validation is actually feasible. A subtly wrong diagram is worse than none.

Now the part that's actually the point — the checking system. The extraction is just raw material; trust is earned, modeled on how Wikipedia itself works. Every fact carries its evidence, not just an assertion: each piece of evidence has a source, a verbatim passage, a URL, and a trust tier. Tiers are fixed — vetted textbooks (OpenStax) are trusted; PubMed, journals, and Wikipedia are "solid" (good for breadth, never primary authority); general web and **every AI model** are permanently low-tier candidates. Consensus is *derived*, never declared: one source is "unverified," two genuinely independent sources that agree is "agreement" (a same-publisher flag stops two OpenStax books counting as two votes), and any refutation flips it to "conflict" and into a human queue. Disagreement must be sourced — a refutation without a citation is rejected, which is what keeps it from devolving into vibes. And "verified" is reachable only by a human signing off: signatures are pinned to a hash of the exact content signed, so if the fact later changes, the signature is automatically invalidated. The model may propose, cross-check, and triage; it may not decide truth, raise its own tier, or sign. Those are human acts by construction.

So the ask to a physicist is small and honest: here's a deterministically extracted slice of Wikipedia's own definitions and link structure for your field; nothing here is asserted as true; flag what's wrong, and sign what's right. The corrections become trusted-tier evidence; the signatures become the verification layer.
